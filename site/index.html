Your website looks great. To you.

Ansy is a free desktop browser that lets you experience your own site the way people with low vision, colour blindness, motor, hearing or attention difficulties do. It brings the tools they rely on too, like screen readers, zoom and keyboard-only navigation. Spot what breaks, then hand your developer a clear report.

Try the idea

Same shop. Seven ways of seeing it.

Below is a made-up online shop. Its designer used colour to say "on sale", "in stock" and "you typed that wrong". Switch how you see it and watch what survives.

See it with

How the designer sees it. A red sale price, a green dot for "in stock" and an orange one for "almost gone", and a red border because the email address has a typo. Now pick another way of seeing it.

Protanopia, no red cones. The red sale price, the green and orange dots and the red error border all slide toward the same muddy yellow and brown. Colour was the only signal, so the signal is gone.

Deuteranopia, no green cones. Red–green colour blindness affects roughly 1 in 12 men. "In stock" and "almost gone" now look identical, and the sale price no longer stands out from the regular one.

Tritanopia, no blue cones. Rare, and it hits different colours: the sage vase and the green button shift toward teal and grey, while the reds survive. Every type of colour blindness breaks a different part of your design.

No colour at all. The extreme case, and a good stress test. "Sale", "in stock" and "error" become three grey shapes that mean nothing. A word, an icon or an underline would have carried the message.

Blurred vision. Uncorrected eyesight, cataracts, or simply forgotten glasses. Small grey text goes first: the old price and the stock labels are unreadable. Big, bold, high-contrast text survives.

Low contrast sensitivity. Common with age and in glare on a phone outdoors. Pale text and thin outlines fade into the background, and the error border is nearly invisible.

Nineteen experiences. One switch each.

Each experience changes how your site looks, sounds or responds, so you can feel the barrier yourself. Where people use a tool to cope, Ansy gives you that tool too. The few that can only give a rough impression say so, and name the tool that person really uses.

How it works

From "huh" to "fixed" in four steps.

  1. Open your site

    Type your address into Ansy, like any browser. Your live site, your staging site, or a page your developer just sent you.

  2. Switch on an experience

    Pick one, or follow a short guided task like "buy something using only the keyboard".

  3. Note what breaks

    Save a finding in one click: a screenshot, your own words, and the rule it breaks.

  4. Hand it over, then re-test

    Export a PDF or plain-text report for whoever builds your site. When they say it's fixed, check again: for a keyboard or screen reader problem, Ansy presses the same keys again and tells you whether it's gone.

Straight talk

A way to see the problem. Not a magic fix.

The accessibility industry is full of widgets that promise compliance with one line of code. They don't deliver it. Ansy promises less, and means it.

Ansy is

  • A testing and empathy tool. It runs on your computer and changes only what you see.
  • A shared language with your developer. Findings come with screenshots and the exact rule, not a vague "it feels off".
  • A first step. Fixes belong in your site's actual code, made by the people who build it.

Ansy is not

  • An overlay or widget. Nothing gets installed on your website, and your visitors never see Ansy.
  • A compliance certificate. Automated checks catch only part of the problems. A clean scan is not a legal guarantee.
  • Lived experience. A simulation is a glimpse. Disabled people who use your site every day are the real experts, so listen to them.

FAQ

Fair questions.

Who is Ansy for?

Business owners, marketers and product people who aren't accessibility experts, and don't want to become one just to know whether their site works for everyone. Developers and designers are welcome too.

Do I need any technical skills?

No. If you can use a web browser, you can use Ansy. Every experience explains, in plain language, what you're looking at and what to look for.

Does it change my website?

Never. Ansy only changes what you see and hear on your own computer. Your site, and what your visitors see, stay exactly as they are.

Why a desktop app and not a browser extension?

Owning the whole browser lets Ansy use the engine's built-in colour-vision emulation, real high-contrast rendering and the page's accessibility tree, things an extension can't fully reach. A companion extension may follow later.

Can a screen reader simulation replace testing with blind users?

No, and we'd be suspicious of anyone who says otherwise. Ansy gives you a feel for the problem and a vocabulary for it. For anything that matters, test with the people who use assistive technology every day.

What happens to my findings?

They stay on your computer until you export them. Reports can include an optional "Ask Slikk.Dev to fix this" link, which you can switch off. Send the report to any developer you like.

Who makes Ansy?

Slikk.Dev, a web development studio that builds accessibility into the code instead of bolting it on. Ansy's code is MIT-licensed. The Slikk.Dev name and logo are trademarks and stay reserved.

When can I download it?

Soon. To hear the moment it ships, open the GitHub repository, choose Watch, then Custom, then Releases.
